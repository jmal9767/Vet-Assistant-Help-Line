# Legal Scope Guide — Veterinary Assistant Help Line

> **This is general information, not legal advice.** Scope-of-practice rules are set
> by each state's veterinary practice act and enforced by the state veterinary
> medical board. Before operating, look up your state's practice act and, if you
> can, confirm your boundaries with your supervising veterinarian or the state board.

## The one rule that protects everything

**Only a licensed veterinarian may practice veterinary medicine.** Practicing
without a license — even for free, even online, even with good intentions — is
illegal in every U.S. state. "Practicing" generally includes diagnosing,
prognosing, treating, prescribing, and performing surgery on animals.

As a veterinary assistant you may share **general educational information** and
help people **decide when to seek professional care**. You may never provide the
things listed under "Never" below.

## ✅ Within scope (safe to do)

- Share general, published pet-care information: feeding routines, grooming,
  exercise, enrichment, litter habits, socialization, crate training basics.
- Explain what routine veterinary care typically involves (wellness exams,
  vaccine schedules exist, parasite prevention exists) — without recommending
  specific products, doses, or schedules for a specific animal.
- Help owners recognize **red-flag symptoms** and urge them to see a vet.
- Help owners prepare for a vet visit: what to observe, what to write down,
  what questions to ask.
- Point people to authoritative resources: their own veterinarian, ASPCA Animal
  Poison Control (888-426-4435), local emergency clinics, AVMA/AAHA public
  education pages.
- Share your role clearly: "I'm a veterinary assistant, not a veterinarian."

## ❌ Never (out of scope — refer to a licensed veterinarian)

- **Diagnose** — never name what condition a specific animal has, even hedged
  ("it's probably just…"). Describing possibilities still steers a diagnosis.
- **Prescribe or dose** — never recommend a medication, supplement, or dose for
  a specific animal. This includes over-the-counter and "natural" products
  (many are toxic to pets at the wrong dose or species).
- **Treat** — never give treatment plans, wound-care instructions for a specific
  injury, or home-remedy protocols.
- **Prognose** — never predict outcomes ("she'll be fine," "that's not serious").
- **Discourage veterinary care** — never tell someone they *don't* need to see a
  vet. The safe framings are "that sounds like something a vet should look at"
  or "if you're worried, a vet visit is never wrong."
- **Interpret diagnostics** — never read lab results, X-rays, or medications
  lists and offer conclusions.
- Imply a veterinarian is involved, use "Dr.," or let anyone believe they are
  receiving veterinary medical advice.

## Gray areas — default to referral

| Situation | Do this |
|---|---|
| "What dose of Benadryl can I give?" | Refuse the dose; refer to their vet (dosing = prescribing). |
| "Is this rash serious?" (with photo) | Don't assess it; describe the general rule: skin changes warrant a vet exam. |
| "My vet said X, is that right?" | Never second-guess a veterinarian's advice; encourage them to ask their vet follow-up questions. |
| "I can't afford a vet, what can I do at home?" | Sympathize, refer to low-cost clinics, shelters with vet services, CareCredit/payment plans — not home treatment. |
| Nutrition question for a *sick* pet | Sick-pet nutrition is treatment; refer out. General healthy-pet feeding info is fine. |

## Operating protections (do all of these)

1. **Disclaimer on everything** — the website footer, the intake form checkbox,
   and the bottom of every reply you send (the templates include it).
2. **Written answers only** — replying by email instead of live phone calls
   gives you time to check scope before sending, and creates a record.
3. **Keep records** — save every question and your reply (your sent-mail folder
   does this automatically). If a complaint ever arises, your records show you
   stayed in scope and referred out.
4. **Use the templates** — `docs/RESPONSE_TEMPLATES.md` keeps wording consistent
   and pre-vetted.
5. **When in doubt, refer out.** A referral is never the wrong answer. There is
   no situation in which you get in trouble for saying "please see a vet."
6. **Don't charge for advice** while operating solo. Charging strengthens any
   claim that you were practicing; a free educational service is the safer
   posture. (Verify with your state before ever monetizing.)

## If someone describes an emergency

Stop everything and send the emergency template immediately: emergency vet
locator + poison control number. Do not attempt to talk them through first aid
for the specific situation.
